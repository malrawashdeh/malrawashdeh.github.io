The Logarithmic Ceiling: Dose Escalation and the Limits of Durability in Intravitreal Anti-VEGF Therapy: A Narrative Review

Mohammad Alrawashdeh

Department of Ophthalmology, University Hospital Heidelberg, Heidelberg, Germany
Preprint, August 18, 2026 · Full text (PDF) · SSRN version

Abstract

Aflibercept 8 mg delivers a fourfold higher molar dose than the 2 mg formulation and has been evaluated in neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), diabetic macular oedema (DME), polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), and, most recently, macular oedema secondary to retinal vein occlusion. Interval extension is broadly similar across the three indications for which mature ninety-six-week data exist, despite substantial differences in the degree to which their exudation is VEGF-dependent. This review argues that such similarity is the expected result rather than a coincidence, and that its implications for drug development are more important than its implications for prescribing. Under first-order elimination at fixed clearance, the additional time a drug spends above any suppression threshold following a dose increase of factor F is t½ × log₂(F). The threshold cancels, so the increment purchased is a property of the molecule and the dose ratio, not of the disease. Two consequences follow. First, comparable durability across pathophysiologically distinct conditions requires no disease-specific explanation. Second, the relationship is logarithmic: the fourfold increase from 2 mg to 8 mg purchases approximately two elimination half-lives, a further doubling to 16 mg would purchase one, and doubling the eight-week interval by molar dose alone would require roughly thirty-fold the standard dose. Observed extension substantially exceeds this prediction, and the leading explanation, derived from pharmacokinetic modelling rather than from direct measurement, attributes much of the excess to reduced ocular clearance of the concentrated formulation rather than to the molar increase itself. On that decomposition the molar step accounts for between a third and a half of the durability actually achieved. That explanation does not rescue dose escalation. It relocates the operative variable from how much drug is given to how long it persists. Both readings converge on the same conclusion: molar escalation is an exhausted lever, and further durability must come from altering half-life through delivery or from altering the threshold through mechanism.
